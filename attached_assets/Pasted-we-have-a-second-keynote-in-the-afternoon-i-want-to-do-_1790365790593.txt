Opus is motivated by my deeply embedded desire to help patients. As a doctor, I I learned the power of humility and humanity in the face of devastating diagnoses that threatened patients and families. In the most heartbreaking cases, options were so severely limited, all I could offer was supportive care, compelling me to work towards better solutions. As an industry leader, I witnessed how fiduciary obligations can keep great innovations from the patients for whom the businesses are meant to serve. 

Hundreds of millions of people around the world, mostly newborns, infants, and toddlers, suffer from genetic conditions that our healthcare and industrial innovation systems were never designed to address. As newborn genomic screening and sequencing are adopted, the numbers are rising. Meanwhile, genetic bioengineering is translating breakthroughs into medicines. Lucky babies have been treated with bespoke interventions. So why aren’t we racing towards eradicating all genetic diseases? 
First, research and development across institutes and industry is moated. Second, knowledge is limited. The human body is an exquisitely complex system. Today, we only understand ~7% of the genome’s function. We know even less about how it works in concert. Current approaches that rely on mining fragmented data across proprietary silos are challenged by data incompatibilities, high costs, and geographical barriers. Third, elite organizations are disconnected from community practices. Real world considerations left unaddressed at the design table leave patients and physicians to overcome implementation challenges on their own. 

We usually to government to tackle complex systemic dysfunction. I joined ARPA-H, the federal U.S. moonshot agency to do exactly this. There, I conceived, designed, and launched THRIVE, $160M ARPA-H program that challenged the world to innovate collaboration itself, embedding patient voices at the helm, conceptualizing strategies as a team versus a hierarchy, and distributing results to communities beyond elite academic centers so patients could access breakthrough therapies closer to home. The original program’s concepts have moved the needle, as evidenced by the various genetic medical centers being built around the world. This is a win I treasure. Unfortunately, rapidly shifting government priorities left me skeptical that THRIVE could achieve all it was intended to in that environment. 

Opus 132 picks up where THRIVE ends. We believe bold philanthropy is well-positioned to catalyze a better future. We seek your partnership and collaboration. Together we can help Opus benefit humanity for generations to come.
